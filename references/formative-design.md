# Formative Design

Use this reference when deciding what an artifact should communicate, what to
add or remove, and how the resulting design should be handed to production.
It supports posters, reports, web pages, slide decks, interfaces, and other
media without treating them as interchangeable canvases.

## Form the communication job

Establish the strongest available answers to:

- audience, viewing or use context, and desired action or understanding;
- central message or research question;
- first thing the audience should notice;
- primary, supporting, and reference information;
- medium, derivative media, physical or viewport constraints, and delivery
  requirements;
- accessibility, brand, institutional, data, and source constraints.

Write the central message in one sentence. Then establish the reading order,
interaction order, or presentation sequence before choosing typography, color,
grids, icons, cards, or components. Strong visual distinctions should explain
an information relationship.

## Select content for available space

When a region has visible unused space, compare these options before adding
anything:

1. add a source-backed result, method, comparison, or explanation;
2. make an existing explanation more legible or traceable;
3. turn an existing dense explanation into a table, diagram, or evidence path;
4. retain the space because it improves hierarchy, pacing, or observation.

Choose an addition only when its communication value exceeds its reading burden
and its source can support the exact claim. For every plausible addition, note:

- the source location or derivation;
- the proposed text, figure, or table;
- the decision it helps the audience make or understand;
- the claim boundary and uncertainty it introduces;
- the space it displaces and the reading cost it adds.

For numerical material, verify the denominator, unit, data and artifact
version, re-aggregation procedure, and generalization limit before designing a
chart. Descriptive counts do not become causal, representative, or statistically
significant merely because they are visualized.

## Create hierarchy and allocation

Make a smallest useful hierarchy:

1. central claim or required action;
2. evidence or result that establishes it;
3. explanation, comparison, method, or consequence;
4. reference detail and provenance.

Give the highest-value evidence the largest and clearest region. Use repeated
components only where their repeated structure helps comparison. If a visual
problem persists, inspect hierarchy, grouping, sequence, terminology, or
content density before adjusting decoration.

Preserve meaning across derivatives while adapting density and sequence. A
poster is not a large paper, a slide deck is not a reduced poster, and a
responsive page is not a fixed canvas that happens to scroll.

## Prepare production handoff

The handoff should state:

- target medium and its constraints;
- content hierarchy and intended reading order;
- source material and claim boundaries;
- layout, type, color, boundary, and component decisions that have an
  information role;
- relevant official or project-specific authorities;
- implementation capability needed and delivery format;
- target-medium validation required after production.

Use compact, role-oriented tokens when implementation benefits from a reusable
visual system. Preserve semantic roles across derivatives while allowing each
medium to change layout, density, and sequencing.

## Handoff checklist

- The central message, primary evidence, and reading order are explicit.
- Every proposed addition has a source and a reason to occupy space.
- Research caveats, uncertainty, and scope remain legible.
- Statistics are reproducible as displayed and carry their limitation.
- The production plan names the target artifact and the verification that still
  needs to occur.

For a later artifact review, retain the selected version, its source evidence,
and the intended hierarchy. Read [artifact-review.md](artifact-review.md) for
the review workflow.
